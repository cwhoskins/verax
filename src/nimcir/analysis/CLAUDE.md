# `analysis` submodule

`src/nimcir/analysis/` answers questions *about* a netlist, from pure graph walks up to full circuit simulation. It depends on `nimcir/netlist` and `nimcir/spice`; neither depends back on it.

**Two result types, because there are two kinds of question.** `Issue` (`issue.nim`) is a yes/no finding — something is wrong, here is what and where; `kind` is an open-ended string, and a check *never raises* on a netlist-content problem, the problem is the return value. `AnalysisReport` (`report.nim`) is a *measurement*: extracted scalars (`Measurement`), the `Issue`s they imply, the full `SimResult` curve behind them, and `assumptions`. A frequency response does not fit in an `Issue`.

**`assumptions` is load-bearing, not decoration.** Every simulation-backed analysis here works by filling in data a netlist does not carry — an assumed regulator soft-start ramp, a package-typical capacitor ESL, a substituted ideal op-amp, an ideal rail source, a leak resistor on an open port — and a number derived from an invented input has to say so where the reader will see it. They are `Assumption` records from `netlist/annotations`, not prose, so a consumer can count them or filter by provenance — `invented()` returns the values nobody actually stated, which is unanswerable while assumptions are strings.

`src/nimcir/analysis.nim` is the hub and exports only what runs with no external toolchain (`issue`, `io_check`, `measure`, `report`). Anything needing a simulator is opt-in per-import, the same rule `spice.nim` applies to `spice/ngspice`: `spice_fallback` (`.op`), `operating` (`.op`), `derating` (`.op`), `dcsweep` (`.dc`), `pdn` (`.ac`), `startup` (`.tran`), `transient` (`.tran`), `opamp` (`.ac`), `contract_eval` (all four).

- **`contract_eval.nim`** — registers the simulation-backed evaluators into `nimcir/contract`'s registry: `dc_voltage` via `.op`, plus harvest adapters wrapping `analyzeLineSweep`/`analyzeLoadSweep`, `analyzePdn`, `analyzeStartup`, `analyzeTransient`, `analyzeCurrent`, `analyzeOpAmpResponse` and `analyzeLoopGain`. **`spice.dc_sweep` is one evaluator for both sweeps**, because `registerEvaluator` allows one producer per quantity and `dc_out_min`/`dc_out_max`/`dc_out_delta` genuinely come out of either -- which of the two ran is decided by a `sweep` condition the role stamps and the setup key carries, never by the quantity, and a claim carrying neither is refused naming the two roles rather than being given a guessed sweep. `spice.transient` declares `settled_v`/`ripple_pp`/`settled_min`/`settled_max` and **not** `final_v`, even though `analyzeTransient` measures one: `spice.startup` already produces that quantity, and `registerEvaluator` refuses a second producer outright. It lives here rather than in `contract/` because `contract` depends only on `netlist` — that is what keeps a design inspectable with no simulator in the build — and an `Evaluator` is a proc-valued data object, so registering from another module is the ordinary path. **Every adapter runs its analysis in measurement-only mode**: each `*Spec` carries limit fields stating the same kind of claim an `Assertion` states, and leaving them live reports one claim twice, possibly at two thresholds. The assertion is the authority. The disabling values are **not consistent** and are verified, not assumed — `targetZ`/`overshootLimit`/`maxPeaking` are `>` tests needing `Inf` (`targetZ = 0` flags *every* rail), `minPhaseMargin` is a `<` test needing `-Inf`, while `currentLimit`/`minBandwidth` are gated on `> 0` and so are disabled by zero. Non-limit issues (`pdn_no_decoupling`, `opamp_no_feedback`, `*_simulation_failed`) still ride along on `AssertionResult.issues` as diagnosis.

- **`measure.nim`** — pure curve math over `openArray[float]`: no netlist, no simulator, no I/O. This is deliberately where the bulk of each analysis's *reasoning* lives, so it is testable offline. `harmonicAmplitudes`/`thd` integrate **directly over the samples' own timestamps** rather than resampling onto a uniform grid: a `.tran` result is not uniformly sampled, and ngspice takes its smallest steps exactly where a clipped or slewing edge is — which is where the harmonics live — so resampling would discard resolution precisely there. The window is the **last whole number of periods**, endpoints interpolated to be exact, because a Fourier integral over a fractional period leaks the fundamental into every harmonic bin and a leaked fundamental reads as distortion the circuit does not have. Fewer than two whole periods, or no fundamental, yields `NaN` rather than a confident zero. Procs taking a frequency axis accept `logX`, and the frequency wrappers (`corner3dB`, `unityGainFreq`) pass `logX = true`: on a `dec` sweep, linear interpolation between decade-spaced samples is visibly wrong near a corner. **Anything not found is `NaN`, never raised or clamped** — "this response never crosses -3 dB in the swept band" is a real answer about the circuit. `gainMax`/`bandEdges` are the **band-referenced** counterparts to `corner3dB`, and the pair exists because the two reference points answer different questions: `corner3dB` measures from the *first swept point*, which is right for a DC-coupled low-pass and is the **stopband** of every AC-coupled stage — a single-supply amplifier's gain-setting leg is capacitively coupled, so its gain at DC is unity *by construction*, and that is what makes one supply work rather than a fault. `bandEdges` references the response's maximum instead and returns both corners, `NaN` on a side that never falls that far (a DC-coupled stage genuinely has no lower edge, and reporting the bottom of the sweep as one would invent a corner). `fundamentalFreq` is where an oscillator's specification starts, and it is deliberately **not** a spectral estimate: an FFT's resolution is one over the window, so a 20 ms window resolves 50 Hz and would place a 1.59 kHz oscillator to within 3% -- useless against a network whose whole job is to fix that frequency. Counting rising crossings of the window's own **mean** instead resolves to the *timestep*. The mean rather than zero, because a single-supply oscillator sits at mid-rail and has no zero crossings at all; with a hysteresis band, because a harmonic-rich wave crosses the mean six times a period and would otherwise read at three times its rate. Fewer than two crossings is `NaN` -- the answer for a circuit that did not start, and it must stay distinguishable from 0 Hz. `gainMax` is named for what it measures rather than "passband" because **no attempt is made to detect the shape** — on a flat-topped band-pass the maximum *is* the passband, on a resonance it is the peak, and guessing which would be a judgement this cannot back up. Malformed *input* (empty or mismatched arrays) is a caller bug and does raise.
- **`io_check.nim`** — the static checks (`checkOutputConflicts`, `checkDCPaths`, `checkRailVoltages`, bundled as `analyzeIO`). **`checkRailVoltages` is a structural finding and not an assumption, and that distinction is the whole point of it.** A rail with a stated voltage gets an ideal source at an isolation boundary; a rail without one is *cut loose as a floating node*, which changes the topology rather than substituting a value. In `designs/ts808_drive.nim` the divider-derived 4.5 V bias initially carried no voltage, and cutting it loose detached the drive stage's gain leg — an amplifier with a gain of 65 measured as a unity-gain follower, 5.9 mdB of peaking against a real 31.6 dB, every claim passing, `exit 0 (clean)`. The floating rail *was* reported, as one assumption among fifteen, reading exactly like the package-typical ESR two lines above it. Being reported and being noticed are different, and the difference is which section it lands in. `sevWarning` rather than `sevError` because a rail nothing simulates is harmless and a design may legitimately not know a voltage yet; the message names the components that would lose their connection, skipping bypass capacitors, since a rail carrying only power pins and decoupling loses little by floating, plus `spice_fallback.nim`'s opt-in `.op`-backed resolution of anything the static walk can't classify. Classification tables now live in `netlist/kinds.nim` and are re-exported from here, so the old `classifyConductivity`/`Conductivity` API is unchanged.
- **`operating.nim`** — one `.op`, solved once and handed to everything after it. **The reason this can be a straight line rather than a fixed point is that at DC a capacitor is an open circuit, so the operating point cannot depend on capacitance** — verified, not assumed: `v(mid)` on a divider with a cap across the lower leg is bit-identical with 100 pF and with 1000 uF. So the `.op` is solved first, every Class 2 ceramic is derated from its *actual* node voltages, and the `.ac`/`.tran` that follows runs against the derated values, with no iteration and no ordering ambiguity. It buys two things. It closes a blind spot that mattered: `spice/models` could previously only read a *stated* rail voltage, so a capacitor on any unannotated node was not derated at all — and that covers precisely the nodes where bias is worst. A 10 uF rated 6.3 V on a 12 V divider's midpoint sits at 95% of rating and came through at its full nominal value; with the operating point it derates to **3.29 uF**. And it removes duplicate solves — `dc_voltage` solved one, `analysis/derating` solved one per claimed rail, all of them the same design with the same answer; `templates/rail.nim` went from three decks to two and `designs/sensor_node.nim` now runs exactly one `.op` for two rails. `voltageAt` returns **`NaN` for a node the solver never saw**, which is a different answer from zero and must stay so. `contract_eval` owns the cache (`sharedOperatingPoint`), **scoped to the `EvalContext`** and stored in its `scratch` under `"spice.operating_point"`. It was previously a module-level `var` keyed on `Design` *reference identity* with no content invalidation, carrying a documented rule instead of a guarantee: a caller that edited a design and evaluated again got the first answer back silently, two designs checked in one process shared one slot, and `verify.readiness` had to bracket every run with `clearSharedOperatingPoint` to stay safe. A context lives exactly as long as one run and a design does not change under a run, so there is no window in which the entry can be wrong — which is what makes a sweep over candidate component values safe, and that is the obvious way to have hit the old bug. Stated limit: **a `.tran` bias moves with time**, so one number from t = 0 does not describe a charge pump's flying capacitor or a rail that is still soft-starting; derating those from this is less wrong than not derating them, and still wrong.
- **`derating.nim`** — the other half of what a rating is for. `spice/models` uses `voltage_rating` to place a ceramic on its DC-bias curve, which *changes* the answer; this asks whether the part is being used within what it can take, which changes nothing and is the difference between a board that works and one that fails in the field. One `.op` over the whole design — **not isolated**, for the same reason `runDcVoltage` is not: a fragment terminated with ideal sources would check parts against voltages the boundary invented. Checks `voltage_rating` against the DC actually across each part, and `power_rating` on a resistor against V²/R. Ceilings are **per dielectric family** because the reason to derate differs: a tantalum is held to 50% because it fails *short* and takes the rail with it, whereas a ceramic's headroom is mostly about capacitance, which `spice/models` now models directly — applying the tantalum rule to ceramics would double-count it. `power_rail` gains an opt-in `maxDerating` param, and the evaluator is keyed by rail (one `.op` per claimed rail, since the quantity reduces to a *worst* over a filtered set and one run cannot serve two subjects). **`current_rating` and `isat` are deliberately *not* checked here**, and the reason is the `.op`: a branch current is not in an operating point for anything but a voltage source, and an inductor's peak is a property of a waveform rather than of an instant. Both are checked by `transient.analyzeCurrent` over a settled `.tran` window instead — see the `current_path` role — so this module stays what its capability says it is: what one `.op` can answer about every part at once.
- **`dcsweep.nim`** — the one analysis here that answers a *difference between two biases* rather than a question at one. Everything else linearises about an operating point or reads one back, so line regulation, load regulation, dropout, a transfer ratio and a DC output resistance were all unaskable however many `.op`s were solved -- which is why `capSpiceDc` is its own capability and not a mode of `capSpiceOp`. **Two procs, split by where the stimulus comes from.** `analyzeLineSweep(nl, source, measureNet, spec)` sweeps a source the design already has -- resolved through `findComponent` *after* flattening, so a source inside an instance works, and identified by `deckDesignator`, so a part that would not reach the deck as a `V` line is refused by name rather than swept as whatever letter it got -- and reports `dc_transfer` (V/V), `line_reg_pct`, `line_reg_pct_per_v` and `dropout_v`. `analyzeLoadSweep(nl, measureNet, spec)` sweeps a constant-current sink it **injects** at the node, reporting `load_reg_pct` and `z_out_dc`; both report `dc_out_min`/`dc_out_max`/`dc_out_delta`. Injecting rather than binding is the same argument `probes.addImpedanceProbe` rests on -- a test load belongs to the measurement, not to the board, and a part put in the netlist to be swept is a part on the BoM and in the layout -- and it is a *sink* rather than a resistor for the reason `spice/boundary` gives: a resistor's current follows the node it hangs on, so sweeping one sweeps the load and the drive together. Neither expands capacitor parasitics (at DC a capacitor is an open circuit, so its ESR is in series with nothing), neither expands source impedance (the default 10 mohm is an *invented* series resistance and a load sweep would add it to the very output resistance being measured), and neither isolates, on `runDcVoltage`'s reasoning. **`dropout_v` is `NaN` when the output never left regulation**, with a `KindNoDropout` issue saying to sweep lower: "no dropout was found" and "the dropout is small" are opposite conclusions and must not share a number -- and the `NaN` is also exactly what an idealised regulator model reports over any range, since an error amplifier driving an ideal source holds its target from an input below it (`designs/lib/adjreg.nim` says so in as many words). The reference the percentage figures are quoted against comes from the end of the sweep where the circuit is *least* stressed and that end differs by what is swept -- high line for a line sweep, light load for a load sweep -- because referencing a line figure to what the circuit does with no supply is a percentage of zero. **The swept range joins the evaluator's setup key**, so two claims over two ranges are two runs: on `templates/dc_sweep.nim`'s 5.1 V Zener reference, line regulation over the specified 6-16 V measures 1.67% and over 3-16 V measures 41.6%, because most of the wider range is below the knee -- both right, and pooling them would report one as the other.
- **`pdn.nim`** — `analyzePdn(nl, powerNet, groundNet)`: decoupling impedance vs frequency. Isolates everything on the rail (`isolateNets`, with the rail *excluded* from termination so it isn't AC-shorted by an ideal source), expands capacitor parasitics, injects 1 A of AC and sweeps. Reports the ESR floor and composite self-resonance (`z_min`/`f_z_min`), the worst in-band impedance, `z_at_<f>` at named frequencies, and — the point of the whole thing — `z_antiresonance`/`f_antiresonance`, the interior impedance *peak* where a bulk cap's inductance rings against a ceramic's capacitance. That peak is created by adding decoupling, is not fixed by adding more of the same capacitor, and is invisible to any ideal-capacitor model. **The target check is bounded on both sides** (`targetFrom` 100 kHz, `targetTo` 100 MHz): below the supply's regulation bandwidth the *regulator* holds the rail up, and it was cut away at the boundary; above ~100 MHz the impedance is set by plane capacitance and package/via inductance, none of which is in a netlist. Checking outside that band flags every board for something no capacitor change can fix. The sweep still runs to 1 GHz so the rise is visible. Two substitutions happen around the isolation and their *order* is the load-bearing part: `expandPinModels` runs **before** it, because `isolate` refuses to traverse an unmodellable component and records it in `dropped`, so by the time it has run there is nothing left to model — once each such part is a per-pin impedance it is passive, and the rail sees what it actually hangs off instead of an open circuit. `expandSourceImpedance` runs **after**, on the fragment, alongside the capacitor parasitics. `modelUnmodelledParts = false` and `modelSources = false` restore the older behaviour of cutting parts away and leaving sources ideal.
- **`startup.nim`** — `analyzeStartup(nl, regulator, outputNet, measureNet)`: rail ramp-up and inrush, via `.tran` only. `measureNet` defaults to `outputNet` and differs whenever there is post-regulator filtering — a series inductance into a capacitor bank is a resonant circuit that can ring the rail well past target, and measuring at the regulator pin would show a clean ramp and miss it. **State plainly what this is**: unless the component carries a `simReplace`, it substitutes an ideal source following an assumed soft-start ramp and measures what the *output network* does — the RC/RLC rise, the overshoot that network produces, and the current the capacitors demand while charging. There is no control loop, no current limit or foldback, no dropout, no load regulation. A rail that overshoots here would overshoot on a real board; a rail that looks clean here can still misbehave for loop-stability reasons this cannot see. Loads that carry no model are stood in for by `models.expandPinModels` before isolation, so the rail ramps into something rather than into an open circuit — with **the regulator excluded**, since it is about to be stood up as a soft-start ramp and the behavioural regulator model would otherwise quietly overwrite the one thing this analysis exists to measure. Every caller that seeds an isolation on a particular part passes it in `exclude` for that reason. **Every rail downstream of the regulator is seeded and left un-terminated too** (`downstreamRails`, a walk crossing `isPassive` parts only, which therefore stops dead at the regulator and never reaches its *input* rail — that one genuinely is up before start-up begins and must keep its ideal source). Nothing downstream is at its nominal voltage while the rail is ramping; that is the premise of the measurement. Leave a ferrite-filtered analog rail out of the seed set and it becomes a boundary termination — a stiff ideal source at 3.3 V — driving current backwards through the filter into a rail still at zero: measured, **2.8 kA of inrush against a true 163 mA**. This is the transient twin of what `IsolationSpec.excludeRails` already exists to prevent in `.ac`, where an ideal source is a dead short; the same field carries both and only the reason differs. Seeding rather than merely excluding is what pulls that rail's own capacitor bank into the fragment, and the regulator really does have to charge it. **A regulator that brought its own model is honoured through every route a `SimReplacement` has, including `build`** -- which is the only one that can express a code model, and which `replaceComponent` would have read as an empty `kind` wired to nothing. Three assumptions that held only for the ramp this analysis invents itself were removed with it: the part is resolved through `findComponent` so a hierarchical path works; the inrush probe no longer assumes a pin named `"+"` (that is the pin of *this* analysis's PWL source and no other model has to have one) but looks for a pin on the output net, reporting **no** `inrush_peak` rather than a zero when there is none -- a switching regulator drives a switch node into an inductor, so the current reaching the rail is the inductor's and no single branch carries it; and `StartupStart` decides where t = 0 is, with `ssAuto` starting from zero with `uic` when the part brought a model. It has to: an operating point finds the state a soft start settles *to*, so the `.tran` would begin at the end of the ramp and report a rise time of zero for a part that takes milliseconds. The `regulator` role's `tRun` is the matching condition, since a run length derived from the *assumed* ramp says nothing about the schedule a part's own model keeps to.
- **`transient.nim`** — three analyses over one `.tran` run, none of which **binds a driving part**, which is the point of it: `analyzeStartup` wants a regulator to stand up as a ramp and `analyzeOpAmpResponse` wants an amplifier and an input, so between them they miss a charge pump, an oscillator and a converter measured cycle by cycle. `analyzeTransient(nl, measureNet, spec)` reports what a node **settled to** -- `settled_v`, `ripple_pp`, `settled_min`, `settled_max`, `final_v` -- `analyzeOscillation(nl, measureNet, spec)` reports what it is **running at** (`osc_freq`, `swing_pp`, `osc_thd_pct`), and `analyzeCurrent(nl, parts, spec)` reports what is flowing **through a part** -- the one question here that is not about a node at all. They share `runWindow` deliberately -- same netlist, same window, same `uic` -- since having the questions diverge in the *simulator setup* as well as in the arithmetic is how one of them quietly measures a different circuit. The design is simulated **as authored, with no isolation**, following `runDcVoltage`'s precedent rather than `analyzePdn`'s: a rail terminated at its nominal by the boundary would be a check that measures its own assumption and passes every time. **The window is the product** -- `tSettle` says when the measurement opens and is *stated rather than detected*, because what counts as settled is a judgement about the circuit (a converter's output settles in a hundred microseconds, a soft-started rail is not settled for five milliseconds) and a detector that guessed wrong would report a start-up transient as ripple. Everything before it is simulated and discarded, via the `.tran` card's `tstart` and again over the samples, which are not the same job: one controls how much data crosses the process boundary and the other controls what is measured. `capacitorParasitics` is on and load-bearing rather than merely more accurate: a switching converter's output ripple is mostly the inductor's current triangle across the output capacitor's ESR, and an ideal capacitor has none. **The derived print step is also ngspice's ceiling on its own internal timestep**, so it sets how accurately the circuit is *solved* and not merely how often the answer is written down -- which is why `runWindow` takes `windowSamples` and `analyzeOscillation` passes ten times what `analyzeTransient` does. A settling voltage and a peak-to-peak barely notice; a frequency does. On `wien_osc`, whose window is 31 whole periods, a thousand samples read 1568.4 Hz where ten thousand read 1573.26 and a hundred thousand 1573.31 -- the ordinary default wrong by 0.31%, three times that design's claim band, in a way that looks like a circuit measurement rather than a setting. **Reaching a circuit that will not start from its own operating point** is `TransientSpec.initialConditions`: node -> volts, emitted as `.ic` and forcing `uic`. An oscillator's operating point is a balance it never leaves, so a `.tran` from it reports a flat line -- a correct answer to a question nobody asked. `uic` is not free and the cost is declared as an assumption on every run that uses it: the operating-point solve is skipped, so every node *not* named starts at zero rather than at its bias. `analyzeOscillation` measures frequency **first**, because `thd` integrates over whole periods of it and a Fourier integral over the wrong period leaks the fundamental into every harmonic bin; a `NaN` frequency raises `KindDidNotOscillate` naming which of the two causes it was -- the node never moved (kick it) or it is moving but not periodically inside the window (widen it). **`analyzeCurrent` is what `current_rating` and `isat` were waiting for**: both keys were registered and set by `parts/policy` and read by nobody, because a branch current is not in a `.op` for anything but a voltage source. It measures `i_peak`, `i_rms`, `i_avg`, `i_pp`, `i_min`, `i_max` per part, plus `i_peak_derating`/`i_rms_derating` -- the fraction of the part's **own** stated rating, `NaN` when it states none, so a part nobody has chosen yet reads as *not evaluated* rather than as infinite headroom. Four decisions. **The subject is a part, not a net**: a net has one voltage every part on it shares and no current at all, only the several different currents of the parts hanging off it. **The three figures a rating is quoted against are magnitudes, because a rating is** -- the sign of a branch current is a *deck* convention (an inductor's follows whichever pin was written first; ngspice reports a voltage source's positive when it flows *into* the + terminal, so a supply delivering 330 mA reads -330 mA) and no claim may turn on it, while `i_min`/`i_max` keep the signed shape. **One run answers every part**, so the setup key is the window and nothing else -- keyed by part instead, `lt8640a_buck` would run its switching model twice at fifty timepoints a cycle to read one set of waveforms two ways, and `Measurement.at` plus `harvestPart` is what stops one part's claim being answered with another's number. And **a three-terminal device is refused rather than guessed at**: `ngspice.branchCurrentVector` maps a deck designator to its rawfile vector, splitting on whether the current is an *unknown of the matrix* (`V`/`L`/`E`/`H` come back as `i(l1)` in the default saved set) or computed from the solution afterwards (everything else is `i(@r1[i])` and must be named in a `.save`, with `all` leading it) -- both spellings verified against ngspice-46, since the manual documents the *vector namespace* names `l1#branch`/`@r1[i]` rather than the ones the rawfile writer emits. A `Q`/`M`/`J` has a collector, an emitter and a base current and "the current through Q1" does not say which, so it comes back with a reason naming the three while the rest of the batch is measured anyway. `measure.timeMean`/`timeRms` are the arithmetic, weighted by *elapsed time* rather than sample count on the same argument `harmonicAmplitudes` makes, and `timeRms` integrates the square exactly for the piecewise-linear waveform (`dt*(v0^2 + v0*v1 + v1^2)/3`) because a coarsely-sampled triangle is exactly what a trapezoid over `v*v` is worst at -- 0.707 against a true 0.577, 22% high, which would read as a part running hotter than it does.
- **`opamp.nim`** — three separate procs, because the three questions cost different amounts and are wrong in different ways if conflated. `checkFeedback` is a pure graph walk: is there a path from the output back to the inverting input through passives only? It accepts *blocking* parts, since a capacitor from output to inverting input is an integrator — negative feedback at every frequency that matters, though an open circuit at DC — and a DC-conductive-only walk would call every integrator broken. `analyzeLargeSignal` is the one that is *not* small-signal, and exists because everything else here is: `.ac` linearises about the operating point, so a clipping diode is whatever conductance it has at rest and never clips — the right model for gain and stability, the wrong one for an overdrive where the clipping **is** the product. It drives a sine and reports `thd_pct`, `output_swing`, `clip_high`/`clip_low` and `gain_compressed_db`. **The stimulus carries the node's own DC bias**, found by running a `.op` on the same fragment first: a single-supply stage sits at a mid-rail operating point, and hanging a `SIN(0 ...)` source on its input would clamp that node to zero and measure a circuit biased into its own stop. Coupling through a large capacitor is the other way to preserve the bias and is worse — the settling time constant becomes the input resistance times that capacitor, seconds for an ordinary input network, turning a millisecond run unusable. Stated limit: the substituted macromodel has no slew limit, no output current limit and no supply rails, so it never clips *itself* — distortion measured here is what the **network** produces, and a stage whose only nonlinearity is the amplifier running out of swing reads as clean. `analyzeOpAmpResponse` sweeps the **closed-loop** response for gain, bandwidth and *peaking*, and reports each of those **twice over, against two different references** — which is the only way to describe both a filter and a guitar pedal with one analysis. `gain_dc_db`/`bw_3db`/`peaking_db` are referenced to the first swept point; `gain_max_db`/`f_gain_max`/`f_lo_3db`/`f_hi_3db` are referenced to the response's maximum. For a DC-coupled stage the two agree and the second set is redundant. For an AC-coupled one the first set is arithmetically correct and answers nothing: the TS808 drive stage reports `gain_dc_db` of 0.001 dB against a real midband gain of 35.8, and a `bw_3db` of 710 kHz measured 3 dB below *unity*, on the far side of a passband that ends at 7 kHz. Nothing was removed when the second set arrived, because for the circuits the first set was written for it is still the right question. `gain_at_f` is the third way of asking and the one an audio stage is actually specified by ("20 dB at 1 kHz"); it is emitted only when `OpAmpSpec.gainAtFreq > 0`, and is `NaN` outside the swept band rather than extrapolated. `analyzeLoopGain` breaks the loop at the inverting input, injects there, and computes `T = -V(A)/V(B)` for a real phase and gain margin. **Phase margin is deliberately not read off the closed-loop curve**: it is a property of the loop gain `Aol*beta` at its 0 dB crossing, and the closed-loop response `Aol/(1+Aol*beta)` is a different function with a different phase — "180 + phase at unity gain" off that curve gives a number that looks like a phase margin, moves in roughly the right direction, and is not one. So the closed-loop pass reports peaking (a real, honest indicator, since a closed-loop peak is what low margin looks like from outside) and the margin comes from the loop measurement. The break is made at the amplifier input because that is a low-impedance-drives-high-impedance point, where inserting a series source does not disturb the loading the loop actually sees; the input source is zeroed so the only excitation is the injected one. Gain margin is `NaN` for a one- or two-pole loop, which approaches -180° asymptotically without reaching it — that is the honest answer, not a large finite number. Pin roles are matched by *name* (`+`/`IN+`/`INP`, `-`/`IN-`/`INN`, `OUT`/`VOUT`/...) because `Pin.kind` distinguishes input from output but not `+` from `-`, and that distinction is the entire content of "negative feedback"; an unmatched component raises naming the pins actually present rather than guessing. Parts *around* the stage that carry no model go through the same pre-isolation stand-in pass, with the amplifier excluded because it gets a macromodel instead. Both `modelUnmodelledParts = false` flags restore the older cut-at-the-boundary behaviour. **`180 + phase at crossover` is a phase margin only if the break opened the loop, and that is now checked rather than assumed.** Breaking at the inverting input opens the only feedback path a plain amplifier has, and for those the answer is exact — a follower and a gain-of-2 both measure 90° against the single-pole model. A Sallen-Key has *two*: its first capacitor returns to the **output**, and that positive path stays closed across the break, so the measured ratio is not a loop transmission at all — its phase starts near +79° instead of 0 and climbs through +180 while |T| is still above 0 dB, and the naive arithmetic reported **450°**, which is not a margin and which satisfies every `minPhaseMargin` a design could state. A stability claim that cannot fail is worse than no claim, so three signatures are tested — the low-frequency phase is not within 30° of 0 or 180, more than one 0 dB crossing, or a resulting margin outside (-180, 180] — and both margins come back `NaN` with a `KindLoopBreakInvalid` issue saying which. `NaN` reads as *notEvaluated* downstream, deliberately not a failure: the circuit may well be fine, it is this measurement that cannot say, and the closed-loop peaking still measured alongside remains a real indicator. **`checkInjectionPoint` refuses to sweep a stage whose input is inside its own loop.** Every `.ac` proc here measures by hanging an ideal source on the input net, and an ideal source *holds* that node — right at an input, catastrophic at a node the feedback returns to. A Wien bridge returns its positive feedback straight to `IN+`, so driving there shorted the bridge out and the tool reported `f_hi_3db` of **289 kHz** for a circuit that does everything it does at 1.59 kHz, with the claim **passing** at exit 0. The test is a conjunction and both halves are needed: the driven net is one of the amplifier's own input pins' nets, *and* the output reaches it through passives — either alone over-triggers, since an inverting amplifier's input net reaches the output through `Rin`+`Rf` but is not an op-amp pin, and a non-inverting amplifier's input *is* `IN+` but nothing feeds back to it. That walk additionally refuses to traverse a rail, the same rule `isolate` applies: the TS808's `vout` genuinely does reach `vamp` through the 47 uF-bypassed 4.5 V bias rail, which is an AC ground and not feedback. A stated `gbw` annotation on the amplifier beats the substituted macromodel's 1 MHz default, which matters more than it sounds — on the 39.8 dB RIAA stage the default cost 0.7 dB at 1 kHz against a 10 MHz NE5534, seven times that network's accuracy budget, and it would have been reported as the circuit's gain rather than the model's. Separately, `detachDrivers` removes anything already driving the net a sweep is about to drive: once a *preceding* stage is stood up as a macromodel instead of cut away, its output is a VCVS, and putting the stimulus across a VCVS output is two ideal sources on one node — ngspice answers with a singular matrix, which surfaced as `key not found: frequency`, an error about a missing vector three steps from the cause. Only stiff drivers are removed, so a stage fed through a coupling capacitor or a series resistor is untouched.

Conventions these analyses read from `attrs`, none of which change a core type: `Net.attrs["voltage"]` (nominal rail volts), `Component.attrs["esr"]`/`["esl"]` (capacitor parasitics, SI base units) and `["package"]` (case size, for the parasitic defaults table).
